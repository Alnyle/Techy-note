This passage explains the concept of **stratified sampling**, which is a technique used to ensure that a sample accurately represents the entire population based on important characteristics.

### **Why Not Use Pure Random Sampling?**

If you were to randomly select a sample from a population, you might unintentionally introduce **sampling bias**. This happens when certain groups in the population are overrepresented or underrepresented in the sample. For example, if you randomly pick 1,000 people from a phone book, you might end up with a gender ratio that does not match the actual population, leading to biased results.

### **What is Stratified Sampling?**

Instead of selecting people completely at random, **stratified sampling** involves dividing the population into **subgroups (strata)** based on an important characteristic (e.g., gender, age, income level). Then, a proportional number of individuals is selected from each subgroup to ensure that the sample accurately reflects the overall population.

For example, if a survey company wants to poll 1,000 Americans and ensure gender balance:

- The US population is **51.1% female** and **48.9% male**.
- The sample should have **511 females** and **489 males**, maintaining the same ratio.

### **Why is Stratified Sampling Important?**

If pure random sampling were used, there would be a **10.7% chance** that the sample would have too many or too few females (e.g., less than 48.5% or more than 53.5%). This imbalance could make the survey results unreliable because different genders might have different opinions on certain topics.

By using **stratified sampling**, the sample is guaranteed to be more representative, reducing bias and increasing the accuracy of survey results.
